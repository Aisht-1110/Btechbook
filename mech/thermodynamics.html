Applied Engineering Thermodynamics: Master Reference & Syllabus Guide

Engineering Thermodynamics is the foundational discipline governing energy conversion, work-heat interactions, power generation, and refrigeration. This comprehensive academic guide breaks down the standard AICTE, Anna University, and State Technical University syllabus into clear theoretical modules, derivations, formula sheets, and university examination solutions.

Unit-by-Unit Theoretical Foundations

Unit 1: Fundamental Concepts & Zeroth Law

Thermodynamics analyzes macroscopic states of matter undergoing energy transfers. The macroscopic viewpoint considers bulk behavior without modeling individual molecular trajectories, making it practical for industrial engine and turbine analysis.

  • Thermodynamic Systems: Categorized as Closed System (fixed mass across boundaries, energy transfer permitted), Open System / Control Volume (mass and energy flow across control surfaces), and Isolated System (neither mass nor energy crosses boundaries, e.g., universe).
  • Thermodynamic Properties: Intensive properties are independent of mass (pressure P, temperature T, density Ï), whereas Extensive properties depend directly on mass or system extent (total volume V, internal energy U, enthalpy H). Specific properties (per unit mass) are intensive.
  • State Postulate: The thermodynamic state of a simple compressible system is completely specified by two independent, intensive properties.
  • Quasi-Static Process: An idealized departure from equilibrium that proceeds infinitely slowly, ensuring the system remains in internal equilibrium at every instant, allowing integration along continuous process curves.
  • Zeroth Law of Thermodynamics: If bodies A and B are each in thermal equilibrium with a third body C, then A and B are in thermal equilibrium with each other. This law forms the physical foundation of thermometry and temperature scales (Celsius, Kelvin, Fahrenheit).

Unit 3: Second Law of Thermodynamics, Clausius Inequality & Entropy

While the First Law asserts energy conservation, it cannot predict process directionality. The Second Law establishes that heat flows spontaneously only from higher to lower temperatures and that complete conversion of heat into work is fundamentally constrained.

  • Kelvin-Planck Statement: It is impossible for any device operating on a cycle to receive heat from a single thermal reservoir and produce a net equivalent amount of work (prohibits perpetual motion machines of the second kind, PMM-II).
  • Clausius Statement: It is impossible to construct a cyclic device that transfers heat from a lower-temperature reservoir to a higher-temperature reservoir without external work input.
  • Equivalence of Statements: A violation of Kelvin-Planck leads directly to a violation of Clausius, and vice versa.
  • Carnot Theorem: No heat engine operating between two fixed thermal reservoirs can be more efficient than a totally reversible Carnot engine operating between the same two temperatures. Reversible thermal efficiency is Î·_th,rev = 1 - (T_L / T_H).
  • Clausius Inequality: âˆ® (dQ / T) â‰¤ 0, where equality holds for reversible cycles and inequality for irreversible cycles. This mathematical relation defines the property Entropy (S), where dS = (dQ / T)_rev.
  • Principle of Entropy Increase: For an isolated system or universe, Î”S_universe = Î”S_system + Î”S_surroundings â‰¥ 0. All real, spontaneous processes generate positive entropy (S_gen > 0).

University Examination Q&A & Conceptual Solutions

Q1: Why is the COP of a heat pump always greater than the COP of a refrigerator operating between identical temperature limits?

A refrigerator's desired output is the heat extracted from the cold refrigerated space (Q_L), yielding COP_R = Q_L / W_net. A heat pump's desired output is the heat delivered to the warm conditioned space (Q_H), yielding COP_HP = Q_H / W_net. From the First Law of Thermodynamics, energy conservation dictates Q_H = Q_L + W_net. Dividing both sides by W_net gives COP_HP = (Q_L / W_net) + 1 = COP_R + 1. Thus, the heat pump COP is mathematically and physically always greater by exactly 1.

Q2: What is the physical significance of Clausius Inequality and how does it differentiate reversible from irreversible processes?

Clausius Inequality states that for any closed thermodynamic cycle, the cyclic integral âˆ® (dQ / T) â‰¤ 0. When âˆ® (dQ / T) = 0, the cycle is totally reversible without internal friction or unrestrained expansions, enabling entropy to be defined as an exact point property (dS = dQ_rev / T). When âˆ® (dQ / T) < 0, internal irreversibilities (fluid friction, rapid expansion, heat transfer across finite temperature differences) generate entropy, reducing the available work output. If âˆ® (dQ / T) > 0, the cycle violates the Second Law and is thermodynamically impossible.

Q4: Distinguish between point functions and path functions with thermodynamic examples.

Point Functions: Depend exclusively on the current equilibrium state of the system and are independent of how that state was reached. Their differentials are exact differentials (d), and the cyclic integral is zero (âˆ® dP = 0, âˆ® dT = 0). Examples: Pressure, Temperature, Volume, Internal Energy, Enthalpy, Entropy.
Path Functions: Depend directly on the thermodynamic path or trajectory traversed during the process. Their differentials are inexact differentials (often written with Î´ or Ä‘), and their values cannot be determined from end states alone. Examples: Work transfer (W) and Heat transfer (Q).

Q5: Why is the Carnot cycle practically unrealizable in modern reciprocating engines?

The Carnot cycle requires two isothermal processes (heat addition and rejection at constant temperature) and two reversible adiabatic / isentropic processes. In an isothermal process, heat must transfer across an infinitesimally small temperature difference, which requires an infinitely slow piston speed. Conversely, an adiabatic process requires zero heat loss, necessitating an infinitely fast piston speed. Alternating between an infinitely slow stroke and an infinitely fast stroke in the same reciprocating mechanical cylinder is mechanically impossible. Hence, the Carnot cycle serves purely as an upper theoretical ceiling for thermodynamic efficiency.
